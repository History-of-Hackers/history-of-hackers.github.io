Susan Headley

Headley is one of the earliest documented practitioners of social engineering as a deliberate, articulated intrusion technique, at a time when public discussion of computer security focused almost entirely on technical controls. Her 1982 television interview, her 1983 testimony before a U.S. Senate subcommittee, and her 1995 DEF CON talk placed a first-hand account of pretexting, impersonation and dumpster diving into the public and legislative record. She is also among the very few women visible in the first generation of American phone phreaks, and her role as a cooperating witness in the early Pacific Bell prosecutions helped shape how the 1980s Los Angeles hacking circle was broken up and later narrated.

Background

Susan Headley was born in Altona, Illinois, in 1959. By her own account she had a difficult childhood and left school in the eighth grade. She later moved to Los Angeles, California, where she was a rock band groupie and worked as a sex worker before her involvement in telephone and computer networks became the activity she is now remembered for.

As a young person she developed an interest and expertise in telephone networks and in circumventing the controls placed on them. Hugo Cornwall and Steve Gold's Hugo Cornwall's New Hacker's Handbook (1990) records that she penetrated the United States telephone system as a seventeen-year-old in 1977 and describes her as "one of the earliest of the present generation of hackers". She adopted the handle Susan Thunder, sometimes written Susy Thunder.

Phreaking and psychological subversion

Headley's distinguishing method was not technical exploitation but the manipulation of the people who operated and maintained target systems, a practice later standardised in the security field under the name social engineering. She described it as psychological subversion: constructing a plausible identity, telephoning staff, and using pretext, authority and rapport to extract passwords, dial-in numbers, internal procedures and system documentation. She paired this with what she publicly called garbology, the recovery of manuals, printouts and discarded paperwork from telephone company refuse.

The approach was significant because the era's institutional understanding of computer security concerned access controls, passwords and physical protection of machines. Headley's account made the point, in plain terms and to a general audience, that an organisation's staff were themselves an exploitable interface. Robert W. Gehl and Sean T. Lawson's Social Engineering (MIT Press, 2022) situates her within that history of manipulative communication.

The Los Angeles circle and Pacific Bell

For a period in the late 1970s and early 1980s, Headley collaborated with the computer intruder Kevin Mitnick, who used the handle Condor, and the phone phreak Lewis De Payne, who used the handle Rosco, on intrusions into Pacific Bell systems and other targets. The group was informal and unstable, and accounts of the period describe substantial personal conflict among its members.

The circle was broken up by prosecution in the early 1980s. In 1981 and 1982 Headley testified against Mitnick and De Payne in a theft case connected to Pacific Bell, and provided evidence against De Payne in a case relating to intrusion at U.S. Leasing. She did so in exchange for immunity from prosecution. John Markoff reported on the sentencing outcomes for the group in InfoWorld in July 1982.

Public testimony and media appearances

In 1982 Headley gave an interview on the ABC news programme 20/20 in which she described techniques for circumventing security systems, including garbology. On 25 October 1983 she testified before the U.S. Senate Committee on Governmental Affairs, whose Subcommittee on Oversight of Government Management held hearings that October on computer security in the federal government and the private sector. In both settings she characterised the motivations of the hackers she knew as curiosity, challenge and intense competition with one another rather than financial gain, a framing that ran against the emerging popular image of computer intrusion as ordinary theft.

In 1995 she returned to a public platform at DEF CON III, where she was billed as Susan Thunder and delivered a talk titled "Social Engineering and Psychological Subversion of Trusted Systems". The talk covered the planning of an attack against an organisation about which the attacker initially knows only a name and a location, and included the use of seduction as an element of social engineering strategy.

Later life

During the 1980s Headley worked as a security consultant and as a professional poker player. In 1994 she was elected to public office in California as City Clerk of California City, California. She subsequently became a coin collector and coin expert. She is married and lives in the Midwest.

Legacy

Katie Hafner and John Markoff's Cyberpunk: Outlaws and Hackers on the Computer Frontier (1991) treated Headley alongside Mitnick, De Payne and others, fixing her place in the standard narrative of early American computer intrusion. She is regularly identified as one of the few women among the first generation of phone phreaks and among the earliest known female hackers.

Interest in her renewed after the writer Claire L. Evans profiled her in The Verge in January 2022 under the title "Searching for Susy Thunder", describing her as "a mythological figure in hacking history". As of 2024 a film based on that profile was reported to be in development. Her career is now most often cited as an early demonstration that the human operators of a trusted system are part of its attack surface, an argument that only became standard security doctrine decades later.
