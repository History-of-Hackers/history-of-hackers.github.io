Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman

Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman co-authored the 1976 paper "New Directions in Cryptography," introducing public-key cryptography and the Diffie-Hellman key exchange, and jointly received the 2015 ACM A.M. Turing Award for this work.

Bailey Whitfield "Whit" Diffie (born June 5, 1944, in Washington, D.C.) and Martin Edward Hellman (born October 2, 1945, in New York City) are the American cryptographers credited with founding public-key cryptography. Diffie earned a B.S. in mathematics from MIT (1965) and worked at MITRE and the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory before pursuing independent cryptography research in the early 1970s. Hellman earned a B.E.E. from New York University (1966) and an M.S. and Ph.D. in electrical engineering from Stanford (1967, 1969), working briefly at IBM's Watson Research Center and MIT before joining Stanford's faculty in 1971, where he remained until his 1996 retirement as professor emeritus.

The two met in the mid-1970s, and from 1975 Diffie worked as a grant-funded researcher in Hellman's Stanford lab. Together they tackled cryptography's long-standing key-distribution problem: how two parties could establish a shared secret over an insecure channel without having exchanged keys in advance. In November 1976 they published "New Directions in Cryptography" in IEEE Transactions on Information Theory (vol. 22, no. 6), which introduced the concept of public-key (asymmetric) cryptography and digital signatures, and described what became known as the Diffie-Hellman key exchange — a method using modular exponentiation of large numbers that lets two parties compute an identical shared secret while an eavesdropper observing the exchange cannot feasibly derive it. Ralph Merkle independently developed related public-key distribution concepts around the same period; Hellman has since argued the protocol would be more fairly called "Diffie-Hellman-Merkle key exchange." Classified work by GCHQ researchers James Ellis, Clifford Cocks, and Malcolm Williamson had anticipated similar ideas as early as 1969-1973, but this was not declassified and made public until 1997, decades after Diffie and Hellman's publication.

Impact and the DES Controversy

The 1976 paper effectively ended the U.S. National Security Agency's de facto monopoly on cryptographic research and laid the theoretical groundwork for RSA encryption (1977) and the security protocols now underlying internet commerce, TLS/SSL, and secure messaging. In 1975-76, prior to and around the paper's publication, Diffie and Hellman also publicly criticized the National Bureau of Standards' proposed Data Encryption Standard (DES), arguing its 56-bit key length was too short to resist brute-force attack — a claim that a 1976 Stanford seminar aired directly to NBS and NSA representatives. Declassified history later confirmed the NSA had in fact pressured IBM and NBS to shorten the DES key from its original design, vindicating their warning. Their sustained public advocacy for strong, non-government-controlled civilian cryptography positioned them as early figures in what later became known as the "crypto wars."

Turing Award and Later Careers

In 2015, the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) jointly awarded Diffie and Hellman the A.M. Turing Award — often called the "Nobel Prize of computing" — for their invention of public-key cryptography, an achievement ACM credited with making secure internet commerce and communication possible. The two had already shared the IEEE Donald G. Fink Prize Paper Award (1981), the Marconi Prize (2000), and the IEEE Richard W. Hamming Medal (2010, with Merkle); Diffie was separately inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame (2011) and elected a Foreign Member of the Royal Society (2017).

Their careers diverged substantially after the 1970s. Hellman remained in academia at Stanford until 1996 and, from the mid-1980s, turned much of his attention to nuclear-risk research and activism, co-founding NuclearRisk.org and co-authoring works connecting interpersonal and international conflict resolution. Diffie moved into industry, serving as a security architect at Northern Telecom, then as Sun Microsystems' chief security officer and a Sun Fellow (1991-2009), later holding visiting academic posts and a security policy role at ICANN (2010-2012). Despite these separate paths, both are almost always discussed jointly with respect to the 1976 paper and the Turing Award, which recognized their collaborative work specifically.
