James Ellis

Ellis framed the possibility of communicating securely without a pre-shared secret. That conceptual move made later public-key constructions possible, although the classified work did not enter the public scientific record until decades later.

James Henry Ellis was a GCHQ mathematician who joined the organisation in 1952. He conceived the possibility of “non-secret encryption,” the idea now called public-key cryptography.

Classified pioneering work

Ellis’s proposal set a challenge for colleagues Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson, whose work produced early asymmetric-encryption and key-agreement ideas. Because the work remained classified, it did not influence the independent public discoveries of the 1970s.

In memoriam

Ellis died in 1997, shortly before GCHQ publicly disclosed this history. His contribution is now recognised as a foundational chapter in modern cryptography.
