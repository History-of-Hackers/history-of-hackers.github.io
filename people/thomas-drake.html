Thomas Drake

Drake's prosecution was only the fourth use of the U.S. Espionage Act to pursue an alleged leaker rather than a spy, and its collapse became a cautionary case study in the risks of using the Act against whistleblowers rather than internal wrongdoing. His account of retaliation for using official channels before going to the press is widely cited as an influence on later disclosures, including Edward Snowden's 2013 NSA revelations. The case also brought public attention to internal NSA debates over mass surveillance programs and the costly, ultimately abandoned Trailblazer data-analysis project.

Background

Thomas Andrews Drake was born on April 22, 1957. He served roughly a decade in the United States Air Force as an airborne voice processing specialist with German-language proficiency, working on electronic intelligence (ELINT) missions, and later analyzed intelligence for the U.S. Navy's National Military Joint Intelligence Center. From 1989 to 2001 he worked as a contractor evaluating software systems for the National Security Agency (NSA), and he became a full-time NSA employee in September 2001, eventually serving in senior roles including Technical Director for Software Engineering within the Signals Intelligence Directorate at Fort Meade, Maryland.

The Trailblazer program and internal dissent

Inside the NSA, Drake became a vocal internal critic of the Trailblazer Project, a multi-billion-dollar signals-intelligence modernization effort. He argued that a smaller, cheaper alternative system known as ThinThread offered comparable analytic capability while including stronger privacy protections for U.S. persons' communications. By 2003, the NSA's own Inspector General had concluded that Trailblazer was an expensive failure, ultimately costing well over a billion dollars without producing a working system, while Drake also raised concerns about the warrantless surveillance activities associated with the post-9/11 program known as Stellar Wind.

Whistleblowing and retaliation

Drake said he first pursued his concerns through officially sanctioned channels, briefing NSA management, the NSA and Department of Defense Inspectors General, and congressional intelligence committee staff. According to his own account and subsequent reporting, this led not to reform but to retaliation: he described being stripped of responsibilities, professionally isolated, and reassigned to lesser duties. In November 2005, after concluding that internal channels had failed, Drake began communicating with Baltimore Sun reporter Siobhan Gorman using encrypted email, providing background information — which he maintained was unclassified — that informed a series of published articles critical of NSA program management and spending.

Indictment under the Espionage Act

In April 2010, a federal grand jury indicted Drake on ten counts, including five counts of willful retention of national defense information under the Espionage Act (18 U.S.C. § 793(e)), one count of obstruction of justice, and four counts of making false statements. The indictment made Drake only the fourth person in U.S. history charged under the Espionage Act over the alleged mishandling of classified information rather than for spying on behalf of a foreign power, following cases going back to Pentagon Papers whistleblower Daniel Ellsberg. Prosecutors did not allege that Drake had disclosed classified material to the reporter; the felony charges instead centered on his retention of documents.

Collapse of the case

As the case approached trial in 2011, the prosecution's position weakened, reportedly following disputes over the classification status of key documents and mounting public and legal criticism. Days before trial, the Justice Department dropped all ten original charges. In June 2011, Drake pleaded guilty to a single misdemeanor of exceeding authorized use of a government computer. At sentencing in July 2011, U.S. District Judge Richard D. Bennett rejected the government's request for a fine, instead imposing one year of probation and 240 hours of community service, and publicly criticized the government's handling of the prosecution as unconscionable.

Aftermath and legacy

Following the case, Drake became a prominent public critic of NSA surveillance practices and an advocate for stronger whistleblower protections, speaking at civil-liberties events and appearing in documentaries such as "Silenced" (2014) and PBS's "United States of Secrets." He received the Ridenhour Prize for Truth-Telling, the Sam Adams Award for Integrity in Intelligence, and the Hugh M. Hefner First Amendment Award, all in 2011. His case is frequently cited alongside those of Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden in discussions of how the U.S. government has used the Espionage Act against national-security whistleblowers, and Drake has said his experience influenced his later public support for Snowden's 2013 disclosures.
