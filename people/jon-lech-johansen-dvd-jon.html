Jon Lech Johansen

Johansen's prosecution was among the first criminal cases anywhere to test whether reverse-engineering a consumer media protection scheme was itself a crime, and his acquittal became a reference point in international debates over anti-circumvention law. DeCSS made playback of commercial DVDs practical on open-source operating systems that the licensed player ecosystem had excluded, linking DRM circumvention to questions of platform interoperability. The case also helped establish the argument, later common in security research, that publishing code which analyses a protection mechanism is distinct from infringing copyright. His subsequent work on Apple's FairPlay system extended the same interoperability argument from optical discs to online music stores.

Background

Jon Lech Johansen was born on 18 November 1983 in Harstad, Norway, to a Norwegian father and a Polish mother. Largely self-taught as a programmer, he left high school during his first year to concentrate on software work. By his mid-teens he was active in the online communities around Linux, media playback and reverse engineering, which brought him into contact with the problem that would define his public reputation: commercial DVD-Video discs could not legally or practically be played on free operating systems.

DVD encryption and DeCSS

Commercial DVD-Video discs are protected by the Content Scramble System (CSS), a digital rights management and encryption scheme introduced around 1996 that uses a proprietary 40-bit stream cipher. The short key length reflected United States cryptographic export restrictions of the period. Because CSS player keys were issued only under licence, there were no authorised DVD players for Linux or BSD systems, and licensed playback software was unavailable to users of those platforms.

DeCSS, released on 6 October 1999, was one of the first freely available programs capable of decrypting commercially produced DVD-Video discs. It was the work of three people, two of whom remained anonymous behind the handles "mdx" and "the nomad" and who reverse-engineered the decryption algorithm; Johansen, then fifteen, wrote the graphical user interface and became the only participant publicly identified. The release drew immediate attention because CSS proved far weaker than assumed: in November 1999 the researcher Frank A. Stevenson published cryptanalytic attacks derived from leaked DeCSS source code, including a method for recovering disc keys from hash values that ran in under 18 seconds on a 450 MHz Pentium III processor. The effective strength of CSS was subsequently reckoned at closer to 16 bits than 40.

Legal proceedings in Norway

Legal threats against websites distributing DeCSS began in early November 1999, and in January 2000 a preliminary injunction was issued in the American case DVD Copy Control Association, Inc. v. Bunner. In 2000 Norwegian police raided Johansen's home. Following complaints by the DVD Copy Control Association and the Motion Picture Association, Norwegian authorities prosecuted him in 2002 under a provision of the penal code concerned with unlawful access to protected data.

The verdict was announced on 7 January 2003, acquitting Johansen of all charges. Prosecutors appealed, and on 22 December 2003 the appeals court upheld the acquittal. On 5 January 2004 Norway's economic crime unit declined to pursue the matter to the Supreme Court, and later that month the DVD Copy Control Association dropped its own case against him. The outcome was widely reported internationally as a defeat for the film industry's attempt to criminalise the analysis of a consumer copy-protection system.

Later work on digital rights management

Johansen continued to publish tools that unpicked media protection schemes. In 2001 he released OpenJaz, drivers for a portable MP3 player. In 2003 he wrote QTFairUse, an early utility aimed at Apple's FairPlay DRM in the iTunes Store, and in 2004 contributed FairPlay support to the VLC media player. In 2005 he co-wrote PyMusique, a third-party iTunes Store client, and worked for the digital music company MP3tunes in San Diego. He moved to San Francisco in 2006 to work with DoubleTwist Ventures.

The company doubleTwist Corporation was established in San Francisco in March 2007 by Monique Farantzos and Johansen, with Farantzos as president and chairman and Johansen as chief executive. Its software, launched in 2008, converted and synchronised media libraries across devices, and a later Android application positioned itself as an alternative to iTunes syncing. In 2009 the company drew press attention for an advertisement placed near an Apple retail store, which was subsequently removed.

Recognition and legacy

Johansen received the Norwegian Karoline Prize in January 2000, and in 2002 the Electronic Frontier Foundation gave a Pioneer Award to the writers of DeCSS, alongside that year's other recipients Dan Gillmor and Beth Givens. His nickname "DVD Jon" became shorthand in technology journalism for the individual hacker positioned against the entertainment industry's legal apparatus.

The significance of the DeCSS episode lies less in the program itself than in what followed it. The leaked source code enabled public cryptanalysis of CSS and informed the libdvdcss library that made DVD playback routine on free operating systems. The litigation it triggered on both sides of the Atlantic became a standard case study in how anti-circumvention statutes interact with reverse engineering, interoperability and the publication of security research, and Johansen's acquittal is regularly cited in arguments that analysing a protection mechanism is not equivalent to infringing the works it protects.
