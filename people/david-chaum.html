David Chaum

Chaum's early 1980s work on blind signatures and mix networks laid the theoretical foundation for both anonymous electronic payments and anonymous communication systems used decades later. DigiCash was among the first companies to attempt commercial deployment of privacy-preserving digital money, prefiguring later cryptocurrencies, and his mix network concept became a direct conceptual ancestor of onion-routing systems like Tor. His research also founded an entire academic community through the International Association for Cryptologic Research (IACR).

Background

David Chaum is an American computer scientist and cryptographer born in 1955 in Los Angeles, California. He earned a PhD in computer science from the University of California, Berkeley in 1982, with a dissertation titled "Computer Systems Established, Maintained, and Trusted by Mutually Suspicious Groups." Chaum went on to teach at the New York University Graduate School of Business Administration and at the University of California, Santa Barbara, before leading a cryptography research group at the Centrum Wiskunde & Informatica (CWI), the Dutch national research institute for mathematics and computer science in Amsterdam.

Mix Networks and Anonymous Communication

In 1981, Chaum published the paper "Untraceable Electronic Mail, Return Addresses, and Digital Pseudonyms" in Communications of the ACM, introducing the concept later known as the mix network. A mix network routes messages through a series of intermediary servers, each of which reorders, delays, and re-encrypts batches of messages so that observers cannot correlate a message's sender with its recipient. This addressed a problem that public-key cryptography alone did not solve: even encrypted messages leak metadata, such as who is communicating with whom and when. Chaum's mix concept is widely cited as a direct conceptual ancestor of later onion-routing and anonymity systems, including Tor, as well as anonymous remailer networks such as Mixmaster.

Blind Signatures and the Invention of Digital Cash

In 1982, Chaum founded the International Association for Cryptologic Research (IACR), which organizes major cryptography conferences and remains a central institution in the field. In 1983, he published "Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments," introducing a cryptographic technique that allows a bank to digitally sign a token of value without seeing its underlying serial number, enabling a user to later spend that token anonymously while the bank could still verify its authenticity and prevent double-spending. This blind-signature scheme became the technical foundation for what is widely regarded as the first practical proposal for digital cash. Over the following years, Chaum extended this line of research with related privacy protocols, including undeniable signatures (with Hans van Antwerpen, 1989), group signatures (with Eugène van Heyst, 1991), and the Dining Cryptographers Problem, or DC-nets (1988), a protocol enabling anonymous message broadcasting among a group of participants.

DigiCash and eCash

In 1990, Chaum founded DigiCash in Amsterdam to commercialize his digital cash research as a real-world electronic payment system called eCash. DigiCash's technology used blind-signature cryptography to let users withdraw digitally signed "electronic coins" from a bank and spend them online without the bank being able to trace individual transactions back to the payer. In 1994, DigiCash ran a public trial distributing one million units of a token currency nicknamed "cyberbucks," and in 1995 Mark Twain Bank in Missouri became the first United States bank to offer eCash to customers. Despite early interest from major companies, including reported discussions with Microsoft and Visa, DigiCash struggled to gain widespread merchant and bank adoption. The company filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 1998, and its remaining assets were later acquired by other firms; Chaum left the company in 1999.

Later Work and Legacy

After DigiCash, Chaum continued developing privacy and election-security technologies. He created Scantegrity, a cryptographically end-to-end-verifiable paper-ballot voting system, which was used in a binding municipal election in Takoma Park, Maryland in 2009, described at the time as the first use of end-to-end verifiable voting cryptography in a legally binding public election in the United States. In 2020, Chaum founded xx network, a project aimed at building a quantum-resistant, metadata-protected messaging and payment platform, continuing his long-running focus on combining cryptographic privacy with usable systems. Chaum has received recognition including the Information Technology European Award (1995), IACR Fellow status (2004), and the RSA Award for Excellence in Mathematics (2010). His combined work on blind signatures and mix networks is frequently credited as foundational to both the cryptocurrency movement that produced systems like Bitcoin and the anonymity-network research that produced systems like Tor.
