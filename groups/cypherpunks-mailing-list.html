Cypherpunks

The Cypherpunks were an informal collective and mailing list founded in 1992 by Eric Hughes, Timothy C. May, and John Gilmore to promote privacy through cryptography, whose members' code, essays, and debates shaped PGP adoption, the Crypto Wars, and the ideological groundwork for Bitcoin.

The Cypherpunks originated in 1992 as a small monthly meeting at Cygnus Solutions, John Gilmore's company in the San Francisco Bay Area, organized by Gilmore, Eric Hughes, and Timothy C. May. The playful name "cypherpunks" — blending "cipher" and "cyberpunk" — was coined by Jude Milhon (Saint Jude) at one of the earliest gatherings. The group's associated mailing list quickly became its main venue, growing to roughly 700 subscribers by 1994 and peaking near 2,000 by 1997, with volumes averaging around 30 messages a day in the mid-to-late 1990s.

The Manifesto

On 9 March 1993, Eric Hughes published A Cypherpunk's Manifesto, the movement's defining text. It argued that "privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age," distinguished privacy from secrecy, and called for anonymous transaction systems and cryptography as tools people must build for themselves rather than wait for governments or corporations to grant. Its closing line, "Cypherpunks write code," became the group's operating ethos: rather than only theorize, members wrote and released working software. Timothy May's related but separate Crypto Anarchist Manifesto and his later FAQ compilation, The Cyphernomicon (1994), extended these ideas toward a vision of cryptography enabling markets and speech beyond state control.

Members and Output

The list drew a wide and technically serious membership. Philip Zimmermann, creator of PGP (Pretty Good Privacy) in 1991, interacted with the community as it grew; Hal Finney was a primary author of PGP 2.0 and later designed the reusable-proof-of-work (RPOW) system. Adam Back, in 1995, wrote a working RSA implementation in three lines of Perl and distributed it on T-shirts as an act of civil disobedience against U.S. export restrictions; he later created Hashcash and co-founded Blockstream. Julian Assange, who joined the list in the early-to-mid 1990s, later co-authored the book Cypherpunks: Freedom and the Future of the Internet (2012). Members built and popularized anonymous remailers (including the Mixmaster remailer and, from 1997, the Cypherpunks Distributed Remailer network built by Jim Choate and Igor Chudov), along with early work influencing tools such as off-the-record messaging and, eventually, Tor.

The Crypto Wars

Cypherpunks were central combatants in the 1990s "Crypto Wars," opposing the NSA-designed Clipper chip (a key-escrow encryption chip for phones, effectively abandoned by 1996 after industry rejection) and U.S. export restrictions that classified strong cryptography as munitions. Cypherpunk-adjacent litigation, notably Daniel J. Bernstein's case (supported by the EFF) challenging restrictions on publishing cryptographic source code as a First Amendment issue, contributed to the eventual loosening of export controls, formalized when President Clinton's 1996 executive order moved commercial encryption off the U.S. Munitions List.

Legacy and Bitcoin

The list's ideas about digital cash, anonymous transactions, and code-as-activism are widely cited as ideological ancestors of Bitcoin; several early cypherpunks (Hal Finney prominently, having received the first Bitcoin transaction from Satoshi Nakamoto in January 2009) were directly involved in cryptocurrency's earliest days, and commentators including Reason magazine have credited May with significant influence on both Bitcoin and WikiLeaks. The original list gradually declined in activity through the 2000s, with successor discussion continuing on other cryptography-focused forums.

Note: some figures cited for list membership and message volume vary by source and should be treated as approximate; the precise year Assange joined the list (late 1993 or early 1994) is given differently across sources.

Timeline

  • 1991Philip Zimmermann releases PGP (Pretty Good Privacy) version 1.0, later closely associated with the Cypherpunk community.
  • 1992Eric Hughes, Timothy C. May, and John Gilmore found the Cypherpunks, beginning with monthly meetings at Gilmore's company Cygnus Solutions in the San Francisco Bay Area; Jude Milhon coins the name "cypherpunks."
  • 1993-03-09Eric Hughes publishes "A Cypherpunk's Manifesto."
  • 1993Julian Assange reported to have joined the Cypherpunks mailing list around this time (sources vary between late 1993 and early 1994).
  • 1994Mailing list subscribership reaches approximately 700; Timothy May compiles "The Cyphernomicon," a lengthy FAQ incorporating his earlier Crypto Anarchist Manifesto.
  • 1995Adam Back writes a three-line Perl implementation of RSA encryption and distributes it as a protest against U.S. cryptography export controls.
  • 1996President Bill Clinton signs Executive Order 13026, moving commercial encryption from the U.S. Munitions List to the Commerce Control List, easing export restrictions; the NSA's Clipper chip key-escrow initiative is effectively abandoned.
  • 1996-1999John Gilmore documents mailing-list traffic averaging around 30 messages per day (December 1996 through March 1999).
  • 1997Mailing list subscribership peaks at roughly 2,000; Jim Choate and Igor Chudov establish the Cypherpunks Distributed Remailer to decentralize list infrastructure.
  • 2009-01Hal Finney receives the first Bitcoin transaction from Satoshi Nakamoto, connecting a founding-era cypherpunk directly to Bitcoin's launch.
  • 2012Julian Assange co-authors the book "Cypherpunks: Freedom and the Future of the Internet."
  • 2018-12-13Timothy C. May dies.
